Not Every "Failure" Means the Same Thing

There is a word in AI governance that does too much work: failure.

Jan 14, 20268 min read1778 words

There is a word in AI governance that does too much work: failure.

A system has a problem, so it fails. An audit finds a risk, so the system fails. A legal team raises a concern, so the system fails. A fairness metric is unstable, so it fails. The word sounds decisive, but it often hides important differences.

Some failures mean: not now, but repair is possible.

Some mean: this claim can be closed as rejected.

Others are not ordinary failures at all. They are red lines, or something close to them. They are not fixed by adding one more document, tuning the model, or promising improvement in the next version.

If all of these are called failure, governance becomes blunt. And blunt governance is easy to manipulate.

Start with a simple case. A company wants to deploy a hiring-screening model. Internal testing looks acceptable overall, but an audit finds that applicants in a certain age group are selected at a much lower rate. Management asks: does the system fail?

That is not a question that should be answered too quickly.

If the disparity comes from insufficient data, narrow validation, or unstable measurement, the right answer may be that the system is not ready. It should not be approved. But it may also not be finally rejected. The institution owes more work: more evidence, a broader audit, review by people with the right authority, perhaps affected-group input, perhaps a restriction on use.

But suppose further review shows something different. The system uses a rule that directly excludes a protected age group. Now the issue is not merely an unstable metric. The problem may touch discrimination law, protected status, or a non-waivable rights condition. It cannot be handled by saying, “We will continue to optimize in the next release,” while the current version keeps running.

Both situations involve a negative finding. They should not receive the same governance status.

Repairable Problems Should Not Be Sentenced Too Early

Many real AI governance problems sit in the middle.

The data is incomplete. The audit scope is too narrow. Error rates are unstable for a subgroup. The appeal notice is unclear. A human review process exists, but no one has shown that it actually works. A vendor submits a report but not the version history. A legal memo does not say the system is unlawful, but it also does not resolve the key rights issue.

None of this supports immediate approval.

But not all of it supports final rejection either.

A more accurate description is that the case is not ready to be closed. The institution still owes something. It may owe evidence, remediation, independent review, legal clarification, or a better procedure for affected people.

This is the status I think of as repairable non-closure. It is not a vague pause. It should have content. What remains unresolved? Who is responsible? When will it be reassessed? What happens if the deadline passes?

The problem is that “remediation” is often written without any of this structure.

Reports say things like: further monitoring is recommended.

That sentence is not necessarily wrong. But it is dangerously easy to use. Almost anything can be placed inside it: fairness concerns, weak appeal rights, missing audit records, uncertain legal authority. Once a problem is put into the drawer of future monitoring, it may never come back out.

So a repairable problem needs a deadline. Without a deadline, remediation is not remediation. It is delay.

If a system is paused because the fairness audit is inadequate, the next audit must be specified. Who performs it? What does it measure? When is it due? Can the system operate before then, and under what limits? If deployment is blocked because the appeal mechanism is unclear, the institution should say who will redesign it and what would count as sufficient.

Otherwise, “repairable” becomes the most convenient word in the room.

Ordinary Rejection Still Needs Precision

There are also cases where rejection is appropriate.

A system fails a minimum performance threshold that was reasonable, relevant to the use case, and set in advance. A version of the system does not satisfy contractual, regulatory, or internal approval requirements. The institution has enough information to say: this claim has failed.

This kind of rejection is not always dramatic. It may not involve fundamental rights. It may not be a legal red line. It is simply a governance conclusion: this claim does not stand.

But even ordinary rejection should be narrow.

What exactly is being rejected? The performance claim? The audit claim? The data provenance claim? The current version? The entire use case?

The answer matters.

If the current version failed a technical threshold, a later version may be tested again. If the audit file is incomplete, a complete audit may change the status. If the intended use itself is unlawful, then the issue is no longer ordinary rejection. It is closer to a block.

In real institutional life these distinctions do not arrive in a clean table. They appear across meeting notes, vendor statements, audit reports, legal memos, and risk registers. That is why rejection reasons need to be written carefully. “Requirements not met” is often too vague.

Ordinary rejection is not a bad outcome. It is better than endless deferral. But if rejection is too coarse, it creates another failure. The institution stops learning. A system is shut down, the file is closed, and the earlier harms, appeal issues, or boundaries for future versions disappear with it.

That is not accountability. It is just the end of a workflow.

Some Problems Are Not “Optimization”

The hardest category is the hard stop.

I do not like using the language of red lines too casually. It can become theatrical. But some failures are not ordinary defects.

If a system lacks legal authority, if it violates a non-waivable rights condition, if its safety risk exceeds what the institution may permit, if the reviewing power is controlled by the party being reviewed, or if affected people have no real path to challenge the system, the problem is not merely “insufficient evidence.”

It should not be softened into continued observation.

Sometimes continued observation is a reasonable posture. But sometimes it is a polite way of saying that the institution wants to preserve the system while avoiding the admission that it has crossed a boundary.

This is why cases like SyRI matter. They show that public-sector AI systems can fail at the level of rights and legality, not only at the level of technical performance. Once a competent authority finds that a use lacks necessary safeguards, the old system does not simply need another iteration. Its continued use, in that form, should be stopped.

A MiDAS-type case can move in the same direction. If a benefits-fraud system cannot provide meaningful notice, appeal, human review, and remedy while directly affecting property and benefits, the problem is not just an engineering backlog. It may mean the system lacks the institutional conditions required to operate in that process at all.

Hard stops are often softened by organizations. They are rewritten as high-risk issues, matters for further assessment, or conditions requiring enhanced oversight. Sometimes that is fair. Often it requires pressure. Who says the system can continue? On what authority? Is the judgment independent? Is there an appeal path? Is there a deadline? Can the stop be lifted, and by whom?

A hard stop is not simply a severe problem. It also requires authority, scope, and procedure.

Who has the power to declare it? Which system, version, and use does it cover? What evidence supports it? What would解除 it? Can the affected institution or affected people contest the designation?

Without those answers, even a hard stop can be abused.

A vendor should not be allowed to define its own blocking conditions. A business unit that wants deployment should not be the body that declares the risk acceptable. Otherwise the same vague language can work in both directions. When the institution wants to proceed, the problem is manageable. When it wants to avoid scrutiny, the problem is too complex to decide.

That is not governance. It is discretion dressed as process.

The Worst Case Is Blurring the Categories

The most common bad governance language is not the absence of judgment. It is judgment that sounds formal but blurs everything.

“The system presents certain risks, but they are manageable overall.”

Maybe true. Maybe meaningless.

Which risks? Manageable by whom? Have the controls already been implemented, or are they promised? Are the risks repairable, rejectable, or blocking?

Another familiar sentence: “Future monitoring of fairness performance is recommended.”

That could mean the system needs further evidence before deployment. It could also mean an unresolved issue has been pushed into the future while the system proceeds. If the issue concerns a protected group, it may be softening something much more serious.

Language matters in governance.

Many AI risks are not solved technically. They are neutralized in documents. A problem becomes “monitoring.” Missing evidence becomes “no evidence of harm.” A weak appeal procedure becomes “human review.” A legal boundary becomes “a matter for further assessment.”

I do not assume all institutions do this deliberately. Institutional writing naturally cools things down, especially when resources have already been spent, a vendor expects delivery, and a business office wants deployment. Severe problems become neutral issues. Neutral issues become action items. Action items slide into the next review cycle.

This is why a more precise status language matters.

Not for elegance. For resistance.

It makes it harder to turn different kinds of problems into the same harmless phrase.

The Distinction I Want to Preserve

For me, AI governance needs to preserve at least one distinction.

Some problems mean: this cannot pass now, but it may be repaired.

Some mean: this claim can be closed as rejected.

Some mean: this system should not continue in this form.

The sentences are close. Their consequences are not.

The first requires evidence, repair, review, deadlines. The second requires a recorded reason for rejection and clear conditions for future resubmission. The third requires suspension, withdrawal, termination, or an independent path for lifting the block.

If an institution cannot say which status it is invoking, it should not claim that governance is complete.

This is why I keep returning to closure. The hard part is not just finding risks. It is deciding what institutional status those risks have. Can the issue be repaired? Is the claim rejectable? Has the system crossed a non-waivable boundary? If we cannot yet decide, who continues the review, and when?

“Failure” is too blunt a word.

Real governance has to split it apart.
