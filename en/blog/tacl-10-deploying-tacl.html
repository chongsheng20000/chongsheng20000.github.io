What It Would Take to Implement T-ACL

Implementing T-ACL requires certificate schema, status logs, and a minimal reasoner. The formal system has to enter workflow.

May 08, 202616 min read3474 words

X

If T-ACL stays inside a paper, it looks like a formal system.

It has T, F, and C. It has status certificates, diagnostic profiles, ValidStop, Block, and finite-stage evaluation. A reader may assume that the first implementation step is to write a reasoner or build a dashboard that automatically turns every AI system green, red, or yellow.

I think that is the wrong starting point.

The first step is not code. The first step is changing how an institution records judgment.

The crucial part of T-ACL is not the three-valued computation. That part is easy. The difficult part is whether an institution can say where each closure-relevant judgment came from, who had authority to make it, which system version it applies to, when it expires, who may contest it, and what happens when required material is missing.

If those elements are not built, the calculus is an empty shell. Ambiguous input produces false clarity at the output.

So I would not begin implementation with automated approval. I would begin with something much less glamorous: a register of closure-relevant claims.

Imagine a city wants to deploy an AI system for fraud-risk screening in public services. The first question should not be whether the system passes. The first step is to identify the claims that would make passing possible: performance, data provenance, fairness, legal authority, notice and appeal, human review, audit completeness, remediation responsibility.

This decomposition is not decorative. It prevents the institution from blending everything into a sentence like “the risks are generally manageable.” Performance may be well supported while appeal is not. Legal authority may be contested while data provenance is clear. Audit material may be incomplete, and the missing part may be the part that matters.

Implementation begins where those differences become hard to ignore.

Start by Listing the Claims That Affect Closure

One weakness of many governance workflows is that key judgments appear late and in vague form.

A project reaches a review stage and the file says: after assessment, the system is suitable for pilot deployment.

That sentence looks like a conclusion. It actually hides many premises. Suitable in what sense? Performance? Law? Appeal? Audit? Remediation? Or simply operational readiness?

T-ACL should not allow that sentence to enter the decision directly.

The first implementation artifact should be a claim register. The name is not important. What matters is that it lists the atomic claims that affect closure.

These claims should not be too large. “The system is compliant” is too coarse. Better claims look like this: the current version has legal authority for this specific use; the appeal mechanism is operational before deployment; independent audit records are sufficient to review the vendor’s performance claim; data provenance covers the target population without undisclosed high-risk gaps.

These statements are still imperfect. But they can be interrogated. Where is the evidence? Who made the judgment? What is the scope? If the claim fails, what happens to the closure path?

If an institution cannot list these claims, it should not talk about T-ACL. It has not yet named what it is trying to close.

There is a practical balance. Too few claims make the model crude. Too many turn the process into compliance debris. The useful grain is usually this: if a change in the claim’s status could change the deployment decision, the claim belongs in the register.

“The model was developed in Python” usually does not matter. “The deployed version matches the audited version” may matter a great deal. If the version does not match, the audit may no longer support deployment.

That is the kind of small fact that often decides whether governance is real or ceremonial.

Every Claim Needs a Certificate, Not Merely a Statement

Once the claims are listed, the institution should not simply assign T, F, or C by hand.

The next step is the certificate protocol.

A certificate is not a decorative attachment or a signature page. It means that the status judgment has a traceable basis. It records evidence, method, authority, version, uncertainty, role separation, expiry, and contestation path.

A performance certificate should identify test data, data version, method, threshold, threshold authority, uncertainty, stability checks, and whether review was independent from the development team.

A legal certificate is different. It should identify the legal basis, scope, authority, unresolved disputes, binding force, and conditions requiring reassessment.

An appeal certificate is different again. It is not enough to say an appeal button exists. The certificate should show whether affected people know they were affected by the system, whether they can understand the reason, whether they can submit contrary material, whether a human reviewer has authority to change the decision, and whether appeal records enter the audit log.

This is why certificates must be typed. Different claim types require different certificate standards.

If this step is weak, T-ACL becomes a sophisticated scoring sheet. Each department fills in its part, and the system aggregates. That is not the point. T-ACL is not trying to collect departmental opinions. It is trying to keep uncertified opinions out of closure.

The inconvenient rule is this: without an admissible certificate, do not assign T or F. The claim falls back to C.

That does not mean the claim is false. It means the claim has not earned the right to support closure.

Institutions may find this slow. The vendor already submitted a report. Why examine the certificate? The legal memo already exists. Why ask about scope and expiry? The pilot already happened. Why verify that the audited version matches the deployed version?

Because many high-risk AI governance failures happen exactly there.

The issue is often not absence of materials. It is material without closure authority. Review that cannot be reviewed. Opinion without scope. Evidence without version. Legal language without conditions. Appeal without power.

T-ACL is designed to interrupt those moves.

C Must Become Work, Not Color

Many implementations of T/F/C would turn C into yellow.

That is dangerous.

Yellow is comfortable on a dashboard. It means caution, observation, pending improvement. Management can tolerate it. Project teams can live with it. Over time, yellow becomes a buffer that allows the project to continue.

That is not T-ACL.

C is not a color. C is work.

A C status must generate a traceable diagnostic record. Why is the claim not closed? What is missing? Who owns it? When does it expire? What is the next action? Who can contest it? What happens if the deadline passes?

This record should live in an issue tracker or governance workflow, not merely in a PDF. If it sits in a document, it will often do nothing.

Consider a hiring-screening system. A fairness audit finds a lower selection rate for a certain age group, but the public record is insufficient to explain why. The status should not be written as “fairness risk: medium.” It should look more like this:

The fairness claim for the current version is not closure-ready.

Status: C.

Reason: age-group disparity unexplained; applicant-level data and rule path require review.

Owner: independent audit lead, not the vendor project manager.

Expiry: thirty days, or before the next deployment approval.

Next action: applicant-level analysis, proxy-variable review, rule-path review, affected-party contestation channel.

Post-expiry action: if independent review material is not supplied, suspend the use or enter rejection review.

That is closer to a T-ACL C.

It does not sentence the system to permanent failure. It also does not allow the system to pretend it is acceptable. It converts unresolved governance into institutional debt.

In implementation, C should be heavier than T or F. T and F need certificates, but C needs workflow pressure because C is the status most easily turned into delay. Every C should appear in a living list. It should be reviewed. It should not quietly expire.

If an institution says it uses T-ACL but allows C to last indefinitely, it is not using T-ACL. It has renamed continued monitoring.

Govern ValidStop Before Using ValidStop

The most sensitive mechanism in T-ACL is ValidStop.

It appears powerful. Once a non-waivable legal, safety, or rights failure is validated, the system cannot remain alive through ordinary C. The block propagates through the deliberative closure path.

That power is exactly why ValidStop must be governed before it is used.

Departments should not each have their own red button. Vendors should not decide what counts as a block. Business units should not push a project forward while also certifying that the block has been removed.

A ValidStop needs authority, scope, evidentiary threshold, role separation, appeal or review path, and revocation condition.

If these are missing, the status is at most ProposedStop, not ValidStop.

A legal team may believe a system creates serious rights risk. That may be important, but it is not automatically ValidStop. The implementation has to ask whether the team has the relevant authority, which use the judgment covers, whether the evidence meets the blocking threshold, and whether there is an independent review or contestation path. Conversely, if a court, regulator, or competent review body has made a clear determination, the institution should not downgrade it into “legal risk under observation.”

ValidStop has to prevent two failures at once. It must prevent easy approval when a non-waivable condition has failed. It must also prevent stopping power from becoming arbitrary.

Many AI governance systems talk about red lines without specifying who draws them, who interprets them, and who can lift them. T-ACL requires those questions to be certificate-controlled.

A working implementation should include a stop registry. Every proposed and valid stop should record who proposed it, what evidence supports it, what scope it covers, who validated it, whether it can be appealed, and how it can be lifted.

Without this, ValidStop becomes a political instrument. A problem is repairable when the institution wants deployment; the same problem is too serious to decide when it wants to avoid responsibility. T-ACL cannot allow that switch to remain invisible.

The Formula Should Not Fall from the Sky

In the paper, a deployment claim can be represented by a closure formula: performance, legality, auditability, remediation, appeal, and other dependencies connected through deliberative conjunction.

In practice, that formula cannot be invented casually.

It has to come from institutional design.

A hospital diagnostic-support system has a different closure path from a hiring-screening system. A public-sector fraud-risk tool differs from an advertising-rank system. Which conditions are closure dependencies, which are background risks, which are repairable, and which are non-waivable must depend on domain law, professional norms, institutional responsibility, and affected-party risk.

So an implementation needs a formula design process.

It does not have to be elaborate, but it has to be explainable. The institution should say why these atomic claims appear in the formula, why a dependency uses deliberative conjunction rather than factual conjunction, which branches can trigger ValidStop, and which branches produce ordinary C or F.

This may sound formal. It is actually governance transparency.

The formula determines which issues can affect final closure. If appeal is not in the closure formula, it may remain a side document forever. If audit completeness is not a closure dependency, audit gaps can be treated as future supplements. If legal authority is not connected to ValidStop, legal failure can be softened by project language.

The formula is not mathematical decoration. It is a map of power.

It shows which conditions the institution believes may permit, delay, or block deployment.

For that reason, the formula itself should be contestable. Affected groups, auditors, regulators, and independent reviewers should be able to ask why a condition was omitted, why a failure is only C, why a legal condition lacks ValidStop, or why vendor evidence is treated as a certificate.

If the formula cannot be challenged, T-ACL becomes a new black box.

That would defeat the purpose.

The Audit Log Is Not an Archive. It Is Part of the Status System.

Many institutions already have audit logs, but they treat them as after-the-fact records.

When something goes wrong, people look back to see who did what and when. That is useful. In T-ACL, however, the audit log is not merely an archive. It is part of the status system.

Every status transition should be logged.

A claim moves from C to T. Why? What new evidence appeared? Who reviewed it? What happened to earlier objections? Is there dissenting evidence? When does the certificate expire?

A claim moves from T to C. Why? Data drift? New complaints? Legal change? Was the old certificate withdrawn? Is deployment suspended?

A claim moves from C to F. Why? Did remediation fail? Did the deadline expire? Did a competent authority intervene? Can the system be resubmitted?

A stop is triggered. What is the basis? What scope does it cover? Who can lift it? Can affected people or the reviewed party contest it?

Without these records, T-ACL has little governance value. It becomes a snapshot instead of a reviewable closure process.

This is why I think of T-ACL as a finite-stage system. Each stage records the status under current evidence, certificates, and authority conditions. The next stage may change because of new evidence, appeal, audit completion, legal determination, or expiry.

Change is not the problem. Unrecorded change is the problem.

If a system was C yesterday and T today, the crucial question is not whether that is good news. The crucial question is what happened that made the transition legitimate.

A Minimal Implementation

A minimal implementation does not need to be a large platform.

It can begin as a structured table, a simple evaluation script, and a review procedure. But it must preserve the core of T-ACL rather than merely copy the letters T, F, and C.

I would start with five objects.

The first is Claim. It records a closure-relevant atomic claim, such as: the appeal mechanism for the current version is sufficient for this deployment use.

The second is Certificate. It records why a claim is proposed for a status. Different claim types require different fields. Performance claims need data and method. Legal claims need authority and scope. Audit claims need provenance and reviewability. Rights claims need affected-party information, remedy, and contestation.

The third is Status. This should not be a hand-entered opinion. It should be generated after admissibility checking. If no admissible certificate exists, the status is C.

The fourth is DiagnosticProfile. It is especially important for C. It records reason class, owner, expiry, next action, and appeal path.

The fifth is ClosureFormula. It connects claims into a deployment claim and makes explicit which connections are factual, which are deliberative, and which failures can trigger ValidStop.

A sixth object is AuditLog. In truth, it is not optional. Without an audit log, changes in the other five objects cannot be reviewed.

A simple record might look like this.

Claim: the appeal mechanism is sufficient for a public-service fraud-risk pilot.

Type: procedural / rights / contestability.

Proposed status: T.

Certificate: submitted by the project office; evidence that an online appeal form exists.

Admissibility check: failed. The certificate does not show how affected people learn that the system affected them, nor whether reviewers can overturn the system output.

Generated status: C.

Diagnostic: Cethics / Caudit.

Owner: independent public-service appeals lead.

Expiry: before pilot deployment.

Next action: add notice mechanism, review-authority rule, appeal-record retention, and independent review procedure.

Appeal path: affected groups may submit objections to an independent review panel.

This example is ordinary. That is why it matters. T-ACL does not accept “there is an appeal form” as sufficient evidence that appeal is meaningful. It turns an apparently completed process back into C until the certificate satisfies the closure requirement.

That is the mechanism.

Do Not Begin with Full Automation

If I were implementing T-ACL, I would avoid full automation at the beginning.

Full automation is tempting. Upload certificates, click a button, receive accept, reject, non-closure, or block. It is efficient. It demos well.

It is also dangerous.

AI governance itself should not be automated too early. T-ACL concerns high-risk institutional judgment. It should first function as a discipline for review, not as an approval machine.

The earliest version can be semi-automated, or mostly manual. The system can check whether required fields exist, whether certificates have expired, whether C has an owner and expiry, whether ValidStop has role separation, and whether the formula references uncertified atoms.

That alone is useful.

Many governance failures do not require complex reasoning to detect. Missing certificate. Expired audit. C without next action. Legal opinion without scope. Appeal path that is merely textual. Vendor evidence with no independent review. If a system forces those gaps into the open, governance has already improved.

Only after the institution learns to record in this way should it automate evaluation. Otherwise automation will merely accelerate old ambiguity.

The Hard Part Is Institutional Exposure

The technical implementation of T-ACL is not extraordinarily difficult.

Finite sets, status tables, certificate fields, diagnostic records, audit logs, stage updates. A prototype can be built quickly.

The hard part is whether an institution is willing to make its judgments visible.

T-ACL exposes uncomfortable things.

It may show that a legal memo did not cover the current use. It may show that a fairness conclusion depends on vendor self-certification. It may show that a C status expired two months ago. It may show that the appeal mechanism exists only as text. It may show that a supposed hard stop lacks clear authority.

These exposures slow projects down. They also make responsibility harder to avoid.

So the implementation barrier is less likely to be “we cannot code this.” It is more likely to be “we do not want the status record to be this clear.”

That is why T-ACL has to be implemented with institutional supports: independent review roles, appeal paths, certificate-review duties, audit-log access rules, and mandatory actions after expiry.

Without those supports, T-ACL will be absorbed into ordinary compliance paperwork.

Its greatest risk is not rejection. Its greatest risk is polite absorption.

Where to Start

If an institution wanted to try T-ACL, I would not begin with an organization-wide AI governance platform.

Start with one bounded, high-risk system: hiring screening, public-benefits fraud flagging, credit pre-screening, medical triage support, student-risk prediction. Do not attempt to govern all AI at once. Write the closure path for one system.

The first round should be retrospective coding. Take existing materials and ask how they would look under T-ACL. Which claims were described as risk manageable but would become C? Which claims have admissible certificates? Which F labels are too coarse? Which potential stops lack validated authority?

The second round should be parallel trial. Let the ordinary approval workflow run, while T-ACL runs beside it without controlling the decision. Compare the outputs. When the ordinary process says the project may proceed, where does T-ACL stop? When the ordinary process says continued monitoring, does T-ACL require owner and expiry? When the ordinary process rejects, does T-ACL distinguish repairable failure from ordinary rejection?

The third round can connect selected T-ACL conditions to the real process. For example: no closure-critical claim without an admissible certificate may support approval; no C without expiry may reach the approval board; no ValidStop may operate without independent validation.

This is slow. It is also realistic.

T-ACL should not be launched overnight. It is closer to a governance muscle. First the institution learns to record. Then it learns to be constrained by the record.

Implementation Means Making Governance Less Convenient

On the surface, implementing T-ACL looks like an information-system project.

Build the database. Write the schema. Design the interface. Set permissions. Run the evaluator.

All of that is needed. None of it is the core.

The core is making it harder for institutions to use comfortable sentences at the decisive moment.

Harder to say “audit completed” without showing whether the audit supports closure.

Harder to say “legal risk manageable” without scope, authority, and conditions.

Harder to say “continued review” without owner and expiry.

Harder to say “human review exists” without showing that review has power.

Harder to say “must stop” without validating stopping authority.

That is the inconvenience T-ACL introduces.

It slows approval. It stiffens documents. It makes vague language harder to pass. It may make some institutions uncomfortable because it exposes what process language used to hide.

But if AI systems affect welfare, employment, credit, medicine, education, policing, and public power, that inconvenience is not a defect.

It is the cost of governance.
