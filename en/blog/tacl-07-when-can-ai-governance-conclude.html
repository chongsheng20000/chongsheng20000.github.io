AI Governance Does Not Just Need More Audits. It Needs a Way to Decide When a Case Is Closed.

The main T-ACL text. It handles the step after audits, legal reviews, and risk assessments: how materials support closure.

AI governance is no longer short on documents.

A serious AI project can often produce a thick file: model cards, dataset statements, performance reports, fairness tests, bias audits, risk assessments, legal memos, human review procedures, appeal mechanisms, vendor compliance claims. Public-sector systems may add impact assessments, procurement records, regulatory responses, court decisions, or legislative materials.

These records matter. Without them, AI governance is mostly aspiration. But once the documents multiply, a harder question appears: are they enough?

Enough to approve a system? Enough to reject it? Enough for an institution to say that the case has been dealt with? Or do the documents show activity without closure, because some necessary condition remains unresolved?

I call this the closure problem.

It is less attractive than words like fairness, transparency, and accountability. But it names a very concrete layer of governance. After evidence, audit, legal analysis, and risk assessment have been produced, how does an institution convert them into a responsible status judgment?

Not every file supports approval. Not every problem supports rejection. Not every “continued review” is legitimate.

T-ACL is my attempt to model this middle layer.

Here, T-ACL does not refer to the computational linguistics journal. It names a proposed closure-status calculus. The phrase is formal, but the aim is narrow. T-ACL does not replace auditing, legal reasoning, ethical review, or institutional judgment. It asks a downstream question: when the governance conditions for an AI system have not all settled at once, may a deployment claim be closed as accepted, closed as rejected, or must it remain open?

This essay explains that interface.

The Missing Layer in AI Governance

Many AI governance tools address upstream work.

A model card describes intended use, limitations, and performance. A dataset statement explains source, collection, and possible bias. A fairness test examines disparities across groups. A risk assessment identifies possible harms. A legal memo interprets compliance exposure. An appeal mechanism describes how affected people may challenge a result.

These are inputs.

But the difficult part is that the inputs rarely settle at the same time.

A system may perform well while fairness evidence remains thin. A legal memo may avoid saying the system is unlawful while also failing to confirm that the appeal path is adequate. A vendor may provide a test report without full version history. An audit may identify a problem, but the institution still has to decide whether that problem is repairable, rejectable, or blocking.

This is where governance language often becomes soft.

The risk is generally manageable.

Further monitoring is recommended.

Deployment may proceed as supporting mechanisms mature.

These phrases are not meaningless. But they often place different statuses inside the same sentence. Missing evidence, open remediation, unsettled authority, incomplete audit records, and non-waivable rights failures can all be cooled down into “future improvement.”

That is the closure gap: governance has more inputs than before, but often lacks disciplined rules for converting those inputs into closure judgments.

More sharply, many AI governance failures do not happen because there are no documents. They happen because documents exist, but the institution closes the case in the wrong way.

Sometimes this is premature acceptance. A system is deployed with extensive paperwork while fairness, auditability, appeal, or legal authority remains unresolved.

Sometimes it is premature rejection. A repairable issue is treated as final failure, allowing the institution to avoid the harder work of remediation, review, or participation.

Sometimes it is indefinite delay. A system is neither accepted nor rejected, and the phrase “still under review” survives without an owner, deadline, next action, or appeal path.

T-ACL focuses on the status problem behind these failures. The institution lacks a disciplined way to distinguish acceptance, rejection, corrective non-closure, and hard blocking.

T, F, and C Are Not Ordinary Truth Values

T-ACL uses three outer statuses: T, F, and C.

At first glance, this may look like another three-valued logic. That is partly true in form, but misleading in interpretation. The statuses are not unrestricted truth values. They are closure statuses.

T means closure as acceptance.

For the present institutional purpose, the claim may be treated as accepted. The phrase “present institutional purpose” matters. A model accepted for one narrow internal use is not automatically accepted for another public-facing use. A hiring model accepted for one role, one data version, and one review scope is not accepted for every role or future version.

T is not eternal permission. It is scoped closure.

F means closure as rejection.

The current claim, version, or use can be closed as failed. This does not necessarily condemn a whole technology. A later version may return. A failed performance claim does not automatically settle a legal claim. F says that the present claim no longer stands for the current purpose.

C means corrective non-closure.

This is the most important status. It means the claim is not ready for acceptance, but also not ready for final rejection. Something remains owed.

That “something” can vary. Evidence may be missing. Evidence may conflict. Audit records may be incomplete. Legal authority may be unsettled. Affected-party contestation may still be open. Data drift may require reassessment. A remedy process may be unfinished.

T-ACL intentionally projects these different reasons into one closure status, C, because at the closure layer they have the same effect: the institution may not close the case.

But C is not meant to erase the difference between those reasons. In T-ACL, a C status must carry a diagnostic profile. It should say why closure is blocked, what evidence or authority is missing, who owns the next action, when the status expires, and whether there is a path for appeal or contestation.

This is where ordinary “continued review” fails. Without ownership, deadline, and next action, continued review is not caution. It is delay.

In T-ACL, C is more like an institutional debt. It records that the institution still owes governance work before the case may be closed.

Certificate-Controlled Closure

The core of T-ACL is not the three-status surface. It is certificate-controlled closure.

This is what distinguishes it from a simple traffic-light model.

In many governance documents, important claims appear lightly.

The system is fair.

The audit is complete.

Legal risk is acceptable.

Human review is sufficient.

The appeal mechanism exists.

The vendor’s evidence is reliable.

These statements may look like judgments. Sometimes they are only declarations. T-ACL does not allow a closure-critical declaration to enter as accepted or rejected merely because someone wrote it.

If a claim is going to support closure, it needs a status certificate.

A certificate is not a ceremonial approval stamp. It is a traceable record of the basis for the status. It should identify the evidence, method, competent authority, system or data version, uncertainty, reviewer independence, expiry condition, appeal path, and dissenting evidence where relevant.

Without an admissible certificate, the claim cannot enter as T or F. It falls back to C.

This rule has practical consequences.

A vendor cannot make a fairness claim closure-ready merely by saying it conducted fairness testing. A business unit cannot close a procedural-justice issue by writing that human review is available. A legal memo cannot convert a contested legal condition into acceptance merely by using the phrase “risk manageable.”

T-ACL does not require every detail to be public in the same way. Some materials may be confidential for legitimate reasons. But even then, the status certificate must identify who reviewed the material, under what authority, with what scope, under what method, and through what contestation path. Otherwise opacity becomes a path to approval.

This is the principle of no certificate, no closure.

Importantly, lack of a certificate does not always mean failure. Often it means C. The institution may supply evidence, complete review, or open a contestation process. But until then, the claim cannot support acceptance.

That is the point. High-risk AI systems often move forward not through complete absence of evidence, but through partial evidence, vendor evidence, internal self-certification, soft legal language, and procedural claims that are never tested. T-ACL does not need to declare all of those false. It asks whether they have earned a role in closure.

Why Governance Needs Two Kinds of “And”

Another design choice in T-ACL is the distinction between factual conjunction and deliberative conjunction.

This may sound technical, but the practical problem is familiar.

In ordinary factual reasoning, “A and B” behaves simply. If I say a dataset covers region A and region B, and it does not cover region B, the factual claim fails.

Governance closure works differently.

A deployment claim may depend on performance, fairness, legality, auditability, appeal, and remediation. When we say all of these matter, we are not merely conjoining facts. We are asking whether multiple governance dependencies are jointly ready for institutional closure.

Consider a system with two unresolved branches. A performance branch has already failed. A discrimination complaint is still being reviewed. Can the institution close the entire case as rejected because performance failed? In one sense the system should not be deployed. But if that rejection erases the pending discrimination review, affected-party evidence, or remedy obligations, the closure is too fast.

Now reverse the example. A competent authority has established a non-waivable legal block. The institution should not be able to keep the old deployment alive by saying that other audit questions remain under review. A hard stop should not be diluted into continued observation.

T-ACL therefore separates factual conjunction from deliberative conjunction.

Factual conjunction handles ordinary co-satisfaction. Deliberative conjunction handles closure dependencies.

This distinction prevents two errors. An ordinary failed branch should not erase unresolved review duties. A validated legal, safety, or rights-based block should not be softened by the existence of other open issues.

This is why T-ACL includes ValidStop and Block.

ValidStop means that a stopping designation has been validated through authority, scope, evidentiary threshold, appeal path, revocation condition, and role separation. Block means that the validated stop propagates through the relevant deliberative closure path.

The important point is not that every negative finding becomes a block. The opposite is true. Blocking requires validation.

This prevents two abuses: uncertified acceptance and unvalidated blocking.

Uncertified acceptance lets systems pass too easily. Unvalidated blocking lets institutions stop, delay, or avoid review too easily. T-ACL constrains both.

The F/C Boundary Is the Hard Part

The most important boundary in T-ACL is not T versus F. It is F versus C.

What does a negative finding mean?

AI governance often writes this too crudely.

A fairness audit finds that a subgroup error rate is high. Does the system fail? It depends. The answer may require the use context, size of disparity, data quality, remediation window, legal standard, and affected-party evidence. If a legitimate remediation process is still underway, the status may be C. The system cannot be accepted, but the issue is not yet a final rejection.

A vendor cannot provide version history. Is that C or F? Again, it depends. Is version history necessary for the present decision? Is there alternative evidence? Is auditability itself non-waivable? If auditability is required and the missing record prevents contestation, the issue may become blocking.

A court finds that a public-sector risk system lacks adequate rights safeguards. That is not ordinary C. If the authority and scope are clear, the finding may support F with ValidStop.

T-ACL’s distinction can be stated this way.

A remediable negative finding is C. It blocks acceptance but requires a diagnostic profile, owner, expiry, and next action.

A claim that can now be rejected is F. It may close the present claim as failed, but it does not necessarily block a larger decision or future version.

A non-waivable legal, safety, or rights failure is F plus ValidStop. In a deliberative closure path, it becomes blocking.

There is also an often ignored case: expired C. If a claim is held open but the review window expires without valid renewal, the institution cannot silently extend delay. It must record an action: reject, suspend, withdraw, or renew through an independent and time-limited procedure.

This matters because real delay often persists quietly. T-ACL treats silence as a governance problem.

Where T-ACL Sits in the Workflow

T-ACL is not another compliance framework.

It does not tell auditors which fairness metric to use. It does not tell lawyers how to interpret a statute. It does not define the correct ethical theory. It is not a model card, a bias-audit tool, a risk standard, or a court.

Its position is between upstream judgment and downstream action.

Upstream are the sources: technical evaluation, dataset documentation, fairness testing, legal analysis, ethics review, audit reports, affected-party evidence, court decisions, regulatory findings, institutional records.

Downstream are the actions: approval, rejection, suspension, withdrawal, remediation, escalation, disclosure, appeal.

T-ACL asks how the upstream judgments support a closure status.

This middle step is where many institutions are weak. They have inputs and actions, but the conversion from one to the other is vague. How does a risk assessment become “manageable”? How does an audit gap become “continued monitoring”? How does a legal question become “in principle acceptable”? How does affected-party contestation change deployment eligibility?

T-ACL requires these conversions to be recorded and constrained.

If a performance claim is T, it needs a certificate. If a legal issue is unresolved, it cannot quietly become T. If appeal is missing, the deployment claim should be C or, where appeal is non-waivable, blocked. If an audit record expires, the status must be reassessed.

It does not replace judgment. It prevents substitution of judgment with vague language.

Three Public Cases

In the paper version, I use three public-record cases: iTutorGroup, Michigan MiDAS, and SyRI.

They should be read carefully. They are not proof that T-ACL improves governance outcomes. Public records cannot establish that. The cases test something narrower: can the interface represent distinctions that binary approval language tends to collapse?

The iTutorGroup hiring case involved allegations that online recruitment software rejected older applicants, followed by settlement and non-monetary relief. The case is useful because it separates legal or rule-level failure from technical audit completeness.

If an old system used a protected-age rule, that may present a serious legal or rule-level block. But the public record may still lack source code, applicant-level data, internal audit trails, and independent technical review. T-ACL does not let one fact erase the other. A legal block does not complete the technical audit file. Missing audit evidence does not erase the legal problem.

This is more precise than saying: discriminatory risk identified; remedial measures taken.

MiDAS concerns automated unemployment-fraud adjudication. The key issue is procedural. A fraud label in this setting can lead to repayment demands, penalties, disrupted benefits, and long administrative disputes. Notice, appeal, meaningful human review, due process, and remedy are not optional add-ons. They are conditions for whether the system is entitled to operate.

In T-ACL, unresolved procedural conditions prevent acceptance. Administrative efficiency does not convert them into T. If a competent authority validates a severe due-process failure in the same pathway, the status may become blocking F.

SyRI is the clearest rights-based example. The Dutch risk-indication system was held legally defective in the challenged context because of insufficient safeguards, transparency, and proportionality. This is not merely a model-improvement issue. It concerns whether the system, in that form, may continue at all.

T-ACL treats such a validated legal or rights failure as a hard stop for the old closure path. A redesigned system can be reviewed later, but the old deployment should not remain alive under the language of continued observation.

The cases stress different points. iTutorGroup shows that rule-level failure and audit incompleteness can coexist. MiDAS shows that procedural justice can prevent acceptance even when technical usefulness is claimed. SyRI shows that rights-based legal failure can block a system rather than merely delay it.

Together they show why AI governance needs status distinctions rather than smoother risk language.

How This Differs from Existing Tools

A professional reader may ask whether existing frameworks already do this.

In part, yes.

Audit frameworks gather evidence. Model cards describe uses and limits. Assurance cases and Goal Structuring Notation organize claims, arguments, evidence, and defeaters. Argumentation frameworks can model support, attack, and undecided status. Many-valued logics handle non-binary evaluation. Workflow systems handle tasks, transitions, and completion states.

T-ACL does not deny any of this. It treats these frameworks as upstream sources.

The narrower question is how their outputs become closure status.

An argumentation system may leave an equity claim undecided. T-ACL asks: does that undecided output put the deployment claim into C? What is the diagnostic profile? Who owns the next action? When does it expire?

A legal process may establish a binding prohibition. T-ACL asks: has the stopping authority been validated? What is its scope? Through which deliberative closure path does it propagate?

An audit report may find that records are incomplete. T-ACL asks: is this a remediable evidence gap, an ordinary rejection, or a non-waivable audit failure?

T-ACL does not replace assurance cases, argumentation frameworks, workflow systems, legal reasoning, or audit tools. It translates their outputs into a closure interface and tries to prevent that translation from being captured by vague managerial language.

This is a narrow contribution. That is why it is useful.

What T-ACL Cannot Do

The limits matter.

T-ACL cannot guarantee that certificates are truthful. An institution can produce polished but misleading certificates. It can disclose selectively. It can simulate independence while the review is captured. These are political, legal, and institutional problems. T-ACL can expose where certificates are being used, but it cannot make them honest by itself.

T-ACL cannot decide what fairness requires. That depends on technical measures, law, ethics, and context. It only requires that a fairness judgment used for closure have evidence, method, authority, uncertainty, and contestability.

It cannot decide what the law is. Courts, regulators, lawyers, and public procedures do that. T-ACL records how a legal status affects closure.

It also cannot validate itself through three public cases. Those cases show representational adequacy, not institutional effectiveness. To test effectiveness, one would need implementation, organizational use, comparison with ordinary approval workflows, affected-party evidence, and internal records.

These limits are not afterthoughts. They define the framework. If T-ACL is presented as a universal governance engine, it becomes implausible. Its best role is as a certificate-gated closure-status interface.

Why Formalize Common Sense?

A fair objection is that much of this is common sense.

Do not approve without evidence. Do not pretend an unfinished appeal process is complete. Do not turn a legal red line into “continued monitoring.” If a problem remains open, say who owns it and when it expires.

I accept much of that objection.

T-ACL formalizes common sense.

But institutional failures often persist because common sense is not embedded in procedure.

Everyone knows continued monitoring should not last forever. Yet it does. Everyone knows vendors should not simply certify themselves. Yet vendor claims often carry decisions. Everyone knows affected people should have appeal rights. Yet appeal mechanisms often exist as text rather than power. Everyone knows legal blocks should not be softened into managerial risk language. Yet project documents often do exactly that.

T-ACL does not invent these intuitions. It places them into a structure that is harder to bypass.

No admissible certificate, no T.

No completed remediation, no quiet conversion of C into acceptance.

No validated authority, no Block.

Once a valid hard stop exists, it cannot be diluted into ordinary observation.

If C expires, it cannot silently renew itself.

These rules are simple. Their usefulness lies in making evasion more visible.

AI governance does not lack grand vocabulary. Transparency, fairness, accountability, explainability, trustworthiness, compliance—the field already has many words. What is often missing is the ability to sit at an approval table and say which claims are closed, which are not, which can be repaired, and which must stop the system in its current form.

T-ACL is designed for that moment.

Not to make governance more mathematical.

To make it less vague.
