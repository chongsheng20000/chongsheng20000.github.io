Accuracy Is Not the Last Question

MiDAS and SyRI are not model-accuracy stories. Once a system enters public power, notice, appeal, and rights protection become deployment conditions.

Dec 19, 20257 min read1531 words
AI GovernanceCivilization
Case AnalysisPublic Sector AIDue ProcessRights Protection
X

I have become increasingly suspicious of the question that often sounds the most reasonable in AI governance: how accurate is the model?

Of course the question matters. If a system has no basic predictive validity, most of the later governance discussion collapses. But many high-risk AI systems do not cause harm simply because they perform poorly on a test set. The more serious harm often begins after the model produces an output and that output is absorbed by an institution.

It enters a benefits office, a hiring pipeline, a fraud-investigation process, a credit decision, a public-risk workflow. It becomes a suspicion, a denial, a repayment demand, a penalty, a file, a dispute. It becomes something a person has to answer.

At that point, accuracy is no longer the final question.

Take Michigan MiDAS. I do not find the case important because it gives us a technically elegant model to analyze. It matters because it shows what happens when automated judgment enters a public-benefits system.

MiDAS was used by the State of Michigan in unemployment-fraud adjudication. In the abstract, that sounds like fraud detection. For the person pulled into the process, it is not abstract. It may mean the state believes you committed fraud. It may mean you are asked to repay money. It may mean penalties. It may mean trying to understand a process you cannot really see, responding to a decision whose basis you do not fully know, and proving yourself inside an administrative system that already has more information and authority than you do.

That is not comparable to a bad movie recommendation.

A wrong recommendation is annoying. A wrongful fraud determination can reshape someone’s finances, benefits, stress, and relationship with the state. In that setting, a model output is not just a prediction. It is attached to public power.

So when I think about MiDAS, I am less interested in the generic sentence “Was the system accurate?” I want to know something more concrete. Did the affected person receive a clear explanation? Was there a real opportunity to appeal? Did “human review” mean anything more than a person glancing at a system output? Could a reviewer actually overturn the automated conclusion? If the system was wrong, where was the correction mechanism? What happened to people who had already been affected?

These questions do not sound as technical as accuracy, but they determine what the system actually is in practice.

Many institutions like the phrase human review. It reassures people. It looks good in policy documents. But human review can be thin. If the reviewer sees only the system’s conclusion and not the underlying evidence, if the workload is too high for meaningful review, if the organizational culture treats the automated output as presumptively correct, or if overriding the system requires extra justification while accepting it does not, then the human layer is mostly decorative.

It is not a safeguard. It is governance theater.

Cases like MiDAS make clear that the real risk of public-sector AI often lies at the institutional interface. How does the model move a person into a process? How does the process turn the model output into a decision? How is the decision communicated? How can the person contest it? How does the institution record, review, and repair its errors?

If these interfaces are poorly designed, automation can make things worse. It does not only increase efficiency. It increases the speed at which errors spread. It may also shift the burden of proof. The institution should have to explain why it suspects you. Instead, you may end up having to prove, under conditions of information asymmetry, that the system is wrong.

That is why accuracy is not the last question. A metric can tell us how a system performed under certain conditions. It cannot tell us whether the system should be placed inside a benefits-enforcement process. It cannot tell us whether an ordinary person has a realistic capacity to resist the decision.

SyRI teaches a different lesson.

SyRI, the Dutch Systeem Risico Indicatie, was a public-sector risk-indication system used to detect possible welfare, tax, and social-security fraud risks. The administrative appeal of such a system is easy to understand. Governments have data. They want to detect fraud. They want to allocate investigative resources efficiently. They want to act earlier.

That story can sound reasonable.

That is precisely why it should be scrutinized.

The District Court of The Hague later held that the SyRI legal framework, in the challenged context, violated Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights. The court was not simply recalculating a model score. The concern was transparency, safeguards, proportionality, and whether the state had an adequate legal basis for interfering with private life in this way.

That matters because it marks a boundary harder than “the model needs improvement.”

Some failures are not fixed by adding more data, tuning the model, or running one more audit. The problem may be that the institutional authorization is inadequate, the rights safeguards are too weak, affected people cannot understand or challenge the system, and public power is operating through an opaque risk mechanism.

In that setting, saying that the system might be useful is not enough.

High-risk systems often defend themselves through usefulness. They save labor. They improve efficiency. They detect risks humans might miss. They help government act faster. Some of these claims may be true. But usefulness is not legitimacy.

A system can be useful and opaque. It can detect risk and place people under a suspicion they cannot contest. It can reduce administrative cost while shifting the cost of error onto people least able to absorb it.

This is the significance of SyRI. It moves the discussion away from tool value and back to institutional legitimacy.

Does the government have authority to combine data in this way? How are risk signals produced? What can affected people know? Can they challenge the system? Is the interference with private life proportionate? If these questions remain unanswered, administrative usefulness cannot become institutional acceptance.

MiDAS and SyRI are different cases, but they belong in the same conversation.

MiDAS shows automated judgment entering a benefits-enforcement process. SyRI shows public-sector risk detection running into privacy, transparency, and rights boundaries. One case is more about due process and contestability. The other is more about legality, proportionality, and rights safeguards.

Both make the same point: an AI system is not just a machine. It is part of an institution.

In the lab, the model outputs a score. In the institution, the score becomes suspicion, investigation, denial, recovery, penalty, monitoring, or appeal. The second half is where much of the harm occurs.

That is why I do not find the “technical evaluation plus compliance checklist” view of AI governance convincing. It is too easy for that approach to imply that once tests have been run, reports have been written, and procedures have been listed, the system has been governed. The hard question is whether those materials are enough to support a decision.

May the system be deployed?

Must it be stopped?

Should the case remain open?

If it remains open, on what grounds, for how long, and under whose responsibility?

This is the problem of closure.

MiDAS-type cases show that when notice, appeal, meaningful human review, and remedy are not settled, administrative efficiency cannot justify approval. SyRI-type cases show that when transparency, proportionality, legal authority, and rights safeguards are not settled, public usefulness cannot justify acceptance.

The differences between problems also matter.

Some problems are remediable. An unclear appeal notice, incomplete audit documentation, or a weak review procedure may be repaired. That does not mean the system has failed forever. It means the system is not ready to be accepted now.

Other problems are not ordinary defects. If a competent legal authority finds that a system lacks necessary rights safeguards, or that its use crosses a non-waivable legal boundary, the issue is not simply “future optimization.” It should block continued use, at least in the challenged form.

This distinction is easy to lose.

In practice, governance failures often occur not because there are no documents, but because the documents blur different kinds of problems. A remediable issue becomes “risk under control.” A legal block becomes “continued monitoring.” Missing audit evidence becomes “no evidence of harm.” A weak appeal process becomes “human review available.”

These phrases are familiar. They are also dangerous. They turn institutional problems into management language. They make open questions look closed.

The lesson I draw from MiDAS and SyRI is simple: when discussing high-risk AI, we should not stop at asking whether the model is accurate. We should ask why an institution is entitled to insert the model into people’s lives, why it may use the model as part of public power or access to important opportunities, and why it should be allowed to continue when the surrounding conditions have not been explained.

This is not anti-technology. It is precisely because these systems can matter that technical metrics are not enough.

The model is only the beginning.

The institution is where the case actually unfolds.
